Why is machine learning so widely used today? The short answer: for a huge class of
useful problems, **learning from data is the only approach we know that works.**
`[00:35]`

## Why we need learning at all `[00:40]`

Machine learning grew up as a subfield of **AI**. We can write explicit programs for
some tasks — finding the shortest path from A to B in your GPS, for instance. But
for the genuinely interesting problems — web search, recognizing human speech,
diagnosing disease from X-rays, driving a car — nobody knows how to write down the
rules by hand. The only way we've found to do these is to **have a machine learn to
do them by itself.** `[01:10]`

Ng's own career traces that arc: leading the **Google Brain** team on speech
recognition, computer vision for Google Maps Street View, and advertising; then AI
at **Baidu**; and most recently **Landing AI, AI Fund, and Stanford**, working on
manufacturing, large-scale agriculture, healthcare, and e-commerce. Today hundreds
of thousands — perhaps millions — of people build ML applications. `[01:50]`

## AGI, briefly `[02:34]`

Looking far ahead, many people are excited about the dream of building machines as
intelligent as a person — **artificial general intelligence (AGI)**. Ng's view: AGI
has been *overhyped* and we're still a long way off (he won't guess whether it's 50
or 500 years). But most researchers believe the best path toward it runs through
**learning algorithms**, perhaps ones that take inspiration from how the brain
works. `[02:52]`

## Why it's a good time to learn this `[02:47]`

A McKinsey study estimates AI and machine learning could create an additional **~$13
trillion of value annually by 2030.** Even though ML already creates enormous value
inside the software industry, Ng argues the larger untapped opportunity is *outside*
it — retail, travel, transportation, automotive, materials, manufacturing. That gap
between opportunity and available skill is exactly why it's a great moment to be
learning this. `[03:38]`
